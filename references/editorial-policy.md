# Editorial Policy

## Source hierarchy

Use the strongest accessible source for each claim.

1. Primary / authoritative sources
   - Company newsroom, engineering blog, security advisory, investor relations, earnings filing, regulator filing, court document, government agency, central bank, statistical office, standards body, research paper, conference publication.
2. High-quality original reporting
   - Major wire services and established outlets with direct reporting, named reporters, transparent corrections, and strong sourcing.
3. Specialist reporting
   - Reputable trade publications, domain-specific outlets, and expert newsletters with original reporting or analysis.
4. Discovery-only sources
   - Aggregators, reposts, social posts, forums, anonymous accounts, SEO pages. Use these to discover leads, not as sole support for important claims unless the post itself is the primary artifact being reported.

For high-impact or disputed stories, prefer at least one primary source plus one independent high-quality report when available.

## Section-specific sourcing

### AI
Prioritize model/provider announcements, technical reports, research papers, benchmark documentation, safety/system cards, regulator material, and independent evaluations. Treat vendor benchmark claims as claims until independently corroborated.

### Technology
Prioritize official product/security documentation, company filings, regulator actions, and original reporting. For vulnerabilities, prefer vendor/CVE/CERT material and clearly state exploit status when known.

### Finance / markets
Prioritize exchanges, company filings, central banks, statistical agencies, regulators, and reputable financial reporting. Use current market-data tools when available for prices. Never infer a market price from an old article.

### World affairs
Prioritize official statements for what an actor claims, but use independent reporting to establish what happened. On conflicts and politically contested events, distinguish verified facts, official claims, and unresolved reports.

## Event deduplication

Consider two articles the same event when they share the same principal actor, action/development, and approximate event time.

Merge coverage when:
- one article is a rewrite or syndication of another;
- multiple outlets report the same launch, ruling, earnings release, attack, policy decision, or acquisition;
- later articles add only commentary, reaction, or small details.

Keep separate events when:
- a material new action occurs;
- a previously reported proposal becomes official;
- verified numbers materially change;
- a second-order consequence is independently significant.

## Ranking rubric

Score each candidate from 0 to 10 before final selection:

- Impact, 0-3: scale of consequences for people, markets, industry, policy, security, or science.
- Novelty, 0-2: genuinely new information versus repetition/commentary.
- Relevance, 0-2: fit with the requested sections and likely usefulness to a general informed reader.
- Confidence, 0-2: quality and independence of supporting evidence.
- Immediacy, 0-1: actionable or time-sensitive significance today.

Apply penalties after the base score:
- -2 if the item is mainly rumor/speculation.
- -1 if it is a minor incremental update to an already-covered story.
- -1 if the evidence is only a low-quality secondary source.

Default selection threshold: prefer 6+; include a 5 only to preserve essential context or category coverage. Do not include below 5 merely to fill space.

## Verification rules

- Verify exact dates, amounts, percentages, version numbers, company names, office-holders, and locations when they matter.
- Seek a second independent source for extraordinary, disputed, or market-moving claims when feasible.
- Attribute forecasts and causal explanations to the source making them.
- Separate “reported”, “announced”, “filed”, “launched”, “approved”, and “took effect”; these are not interchangeable.
- Do not reuse a headline's framing when the underlying source is more nuanced.

## Editorial exclusions

Normally exclude:
- celebrity gossip without broad significance;
- pure opinion pieces with no new facts;
- recycled explainers attached to old news;
- minor product promotions;
- listicles and SEO aggregation;
- rumors with no credible corroboration;
- duplicate market wrap stories that add no new information.
